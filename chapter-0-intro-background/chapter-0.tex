\section{Numerical Modeling of Circuit QED Systems}

Numerical methods are a powerful tool for understanding the behavior of circuit QED systems.
There are a limited number of analytical solutions to the Hamiltonians that describe \gls{cqed} systems.
Numerical methods are, to be clear, approximate solutions.
However with modest computational resources, one can prove invaluable in system design and verifying analytical expressions.
Numerical methods are also necessary for simulating time dynamics, where even fewer analytical solutions exist.

\subsection{Finite Difference \& Spectral Methods}

To model a system numerically, we must first define an appropriate basis. Unsurprisingly, this differs based on the circuit.
In Superconducting circuits there are two distinct Hilbert spaces that emerge.
The first is a standard continuous position (phase), momentum (charge) conjugate pair, as in the simple harmonic oscillator. 
The second is a $2\pi$ periodic compact phase, or position, coordinate and integer valued charge, or momentum, coordinate. This would represent e.g., one unit cell of a Transmon potential.

To model the continuous Hilbert, we must identify a basis that spans possible eigenfunctions $\psi(x)$, which will all be square normalizable, $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx|\psi(x)|^2 = 1$.
The two most common choices are first a discrete position or momentum eigenstates
\begin{align}
    \hat \charge \ket{\charge} = \charge \ket{\charge}, \quad \hat \phase \ket{\charge} = i\frac{\partial}{\partial \charge} \ket{\charge} \qquad \& \qquad \hat \charge \ket{\phase} = -i\frac{\partial}{\partial \phase}\ket{\phase}, \quad \hat \phase\ket{\phase} = \phase \ket{\phase} \,,
\end{align}
which are sampled at a finite number of points.
Another straightforward choice is the harmonic oscillator basis
\begin{align}
    \ket{m}
\end{align}
